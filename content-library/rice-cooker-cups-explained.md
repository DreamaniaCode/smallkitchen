# Rice Cooker Cups Explained: Dry Capacity vs Cooked Yield

Understand rice-cooker cup sizes, dry capacity and cooked-yield labels so you can compare compact models on equal terms.

Rice-cooker capacity looks simple until two products describe it differently. One listing may lead with uncooked rice, while another emphasizes cooked yield. Add a smaller supplied measuring cup and the same number can represent very different amounts of food.

The solution is to normalize the labels before comparing appliances. You do not need a complicated spreadsheet. You need a clearly named unit, the quantity before cooking and the model’s instructions. This article is a specification-reading guide, not a cooking-performance test.

## Identify what the headline is measuring
Read the product title, specifications and manual together. Look for the words uncooked, raw, dry, cooked or yield. If a headline says only “six cups,” leave the comparison incomplete until another manufacturer document explains the claim. A large number with an unclear definition is not a useful measurement.

Distinguish rice capacity from a removable pot’s overall volume, too. A pot may have space above a cooking line for reasons explained in the instructions. Its physical volume does not authorize filling it above the permitted mark. Record the specified cooking range instead of measuring the pot to its rim.

## Check the supplied cup standard
Zojirushi explains that its stated rice-cooker capacities use raw short-grain white rice measured with the supplied cup, approximately 180 mL. Its guidance also notes that other rice types can have different maximums. See the manufacturer’s [capacity explanation](https://store.zojirushi.com/collections/rice-cookers).

As an illustrative arithmetic example, three 180 mL measures total 540 mL of dry measured volume. This calculation explains the label; it does not predict a serving count or a cooked yield. A household’s portion sizes, rice choice and meal composition still matter.

## Do not turn expansion into a universal promise
Avoid choosing a cooker by multiplying every dry quantity by one fixed cooked-yield factor. Grain variety and preparation can change the result. Where a manufacturer publishes a cooked-yield statement, keep it attached to that exact product and the conditions it describes. Do not silently apply it to another model.

Instead, begin with the dry quantity you already use successfully. If you do not have a routine yet, keep your first comparison about supported ranges rather than promised portions. You can refine the amount through ordinary use within the appliance’s instructions after choosing the right size range.

## Build a comparison that stays readable
For each candidate, record five items: exact US model, headline capacity, whether that capacity is dry or cooked, measuring-cup standard and permitted range for your usual rice menu. Add a separate note for the smallest batch. This prevents a large maximum from hiding an inconvenient minimum.

Keep product dimensions in different columns. Two appliances with similar dry capacity can have different bodies, handles and lid arrangements. Capacity answers “how much can it cook under these instructions?” Dimensions answer “where can I store and operate it?” Neither number replaces the other.

## Keep the right cup with the appliance
After purchase, store the supplied cup where you can find it. If it disappears, confirm the correct replacement or equivalent measurement with the manufacturer rather than using an unmarked scoop. A clear unit matters when you are following water lines designed around that unit.

When discussing a batch with someone else, name the measurement explicitly: for example, “one supplied rice cup, using the white-rice instructions.” That small habit avoids a surprising amount of confusion. It also makes recipes, shopping comparisons and your own cooking notes easier to interpret months later.
